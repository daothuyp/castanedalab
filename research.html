Research

Protein quality control, under a microscope and in solution.

Every cell has to decide, constantly, which proteins to keep folded and functional and which to send for disposal. We study shuttle proteins — factors like UBQLN2 that make this decision — and ask a broader question: what governs a shuttle protein's ability to phase separate, and does that predict whether it forms condensates in living cells?

Shuttle proteins as a family

UBQLN2 belongs to a family of shuttle proteins that recognize ubiquitin tags on damaged proteins and escort them to the proteasome for degradation — a family that includes its human paralogs (UBQLN1, UBQLN3, UBQLN4) and the unrelated shuttle protein RAD23. This job depends on two folded domains at either end of an otherwise disordered protein, one that grips ubiquitin and one that docks onto the proteasome.

Mutations that cluster in UBQLN2's disordered middle region cause an inherited, X-linked form of ALS and frontotemporal dementia. We want to understand what these mutations actually do to the protein, structurally and functionally, and why that produces disease decades later.

A second job: phase separation

Many shuttle proteins don't just stay in solution. Under stress, they self-associate and undergo liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), condensing into liquid droplets, and some are recruited into stress granules, a class of membraneless organelle that stockpiles RNA and protein while a cell weathers stress.

We track this transition biophysically, asking what tips a shuttle protein from dispersed to droplet, how disease mutations shift that balance, and what it means for a protein to have two competing jobs.

What governs phase separation, across species?

UBQLN2 is only one data point. To find general principles, we compare it against shuttle proteins that are related by descent but separated by hundreds of millions of years of evolution: Dsk2 in budding yeast, Dsk2 in Arabidopsis, and UBQLN in C. elegans, alongside the human ubiquilins and RAD23. Asking the same biophysical questions across this set lets us separate what's a quirk of UBQLN2 from what's a general property of shuttle proteins — and test whether a protein's intrinsic ability to phase separate in a test tube actually predicts whether it forms condensates inside a living cell.

Retrotransposon-derived proteins and RTL8

A large share of the human genome descends from retrotransposons, mobile genetic elements that have, over evolutionary time, been repurposed into functional host proteins. RTL8 is one such domesticated retrotransposon protein, and it binds UBQLN2 directly. We're working out where on UBQLN2 this interaction happens, whether RTL8 behaves as a client that UBQLN2 chaperones, and how binding RTL8 competes with UBQLN2's proteasomal shuttling role. This sits at the intersection of two questions we care about: how UBQLN2 recognizes what it holds onto, and how retrotransposon biology continues to shape present-day cell physiology.

How we work

We combine structural biophysics with cell biology, moving between purified protein in a test tube and living cells under a microscope.

  • NMR spectroscopy
  • Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS)
  • Circular dichroism
  • Fluorescence anisotropy
  • Confocal & live-cell microscopy
  • Mass spectrometry
  • Recombinant protein purification
  • Phylogenetics & sequence analysis

Molecular dynamics simulations complement this work but are carried out with computational collaborators rather than in-house.
